Mechanical Potato Cannon

A pneumatic launcher built around a rigid chassis and a two-axis aiming gimbal, with aiming coordinates set electronically rather than by eye. The frame carries the pressure vessel and barrel on a bearing-mounted yoke, so azimuth and elevation move independently without the barrel loading the joint.

The pneumatic side is deliberately simple and inspectable: a rated air reservoir, a regulator and gauge on the fill line, a bleed valve, and a fast-acting valve between reservoir and barrel. The valve is triggered by a solenoid rather than by hand, which keeps the operator behind the frame and out of the barrel's plane during firing.

Aiming is closed through a microcontroller. Two 10 kΩ linear potentiometers sit on a handheld panel — one for azimuth, one for elevation. Each wiper feeds an analog input on the Nano, which reads the 0–1023 ADC value, applies a calibration offset and a small deadband, and maps it to a target angle. The angles are driven out to the gimbal servos and echoed to a small display so the coordinate pair is readable before the shot. Firing is gated behind a separate arming switch, so a stray knock on a pot moves the barrel but never releases the tank.

Aiming loop — core mapping

// Replace with the actual sketch — this is the shape of the mapping.
const int AZ_PIN = A0, EL_PIN = A1;
const int AZ_MIN = 0,  AZ_MAX = 180;   // degrees of travel
const int EL_MIN = 10, EL_MAX = 70;

int readAxis(int pin, int lo, int hi) {
  int raw = analogRead(pin);          // 0 – 1023
  raw = constrain(raw, DEADBAND, 1023 - DEADBAND);
  return map(raw, DEADBAND, 1023 - DEADBAND, lo, hi);
}

void loop() {
  int az = readAxis(AZ_PIN, AZ_MIN, AZ_MAX);
  int el = readAxis(EL_PIN, EL_MIN, EL_MAX);
  azServo.write(az);
  elServo.write(el);
  showCoords(az, el);                   // echo to display
}

Acetaminophen Synthesis

A single-step acetylation carried out end to end: reaction, workup, purification, and analytical confirmation that the white solid in the filter really is what the scheme says it is.

Pathway. The aromatic amine of 4-aminophenol is the nucleophile; acetic anhydride is the acylating agent. The amine attacks a carbonyl, the tetrahedral intermediate collapses, and acetate leaves — giving the amide and acetic acid as the by-product. The phenol is left untouched by keeping the conditions mild and aqueous, so the reaction stays selective for the nitrogen rather than running on to the ester.

Reaction scheme

4-aminophenol + (CH₃CO)₂O H₂O, warm ⟶ ~20 min acetaminophen + CH₃COOH

Lab setup. Dissolution and reaction in a flask on a stirring hot plate with a water bath for even heating, then vacuum filtration through a Büchner funnel, a cold-water wash to carry off residual acid, and drying before any measurement is taken.

Safety protocol

  • Acetic anhydride handled in the fume hood — corrosive, lachrymatory, reacts with water.
  • Goggles, nitrile gloves, and a lab coat throughout; gloves changed after any anhydride contact.
  • Water bath rather than an open flame; no ignition sources near the volatile workup.
  • Acidic filtrate neutralized before disposal; solid waste kept separate and labeled.
  • Product is a synthesis exercise and analytical exercise only — not of pharmaceutical grade, and not for consumption.

Purity verification

  • Melting point. Literature value for pure acetaminophen is 169–171 °C. A sharp range at that value indicates clean product; a depressed, broadened range points to residual starting material or solvent, and calls for a second recrystallization.
  • TLC. Product spotted alongside 4-aminophenol and a commercial standard. A single spot co-running with the standard, and the absence of the starting-material spot, confirms the conversion went through.

3D Printing & CAD Design

Parts drawn from scratch in CAD — no downloaded models — then sliced, printed, and iterated until the physical part matches what the sketch promised. Most of the work happens before the printer starts: deciding where a part splits, which surfaces have to be dimensionally honest, and which way the layers should run for the load the part will actually take.

Fully 3D-printed ukulele

A playable soprano ukulele modeled as an assembly rather than a single body. At a ~350 mm scale length the instrument is far longer than any hobby build plate, so the body and neck are split into printable sections joined on registered mating faces — alignment pins and a glued lap joint at the heel — chosen so the seams fall where the instrument is stiffest rather than across the soundboard.

Acoustic considerations drive the wall design. A solid printed shell is too dead and too heavy, so the body is a thin-walled resonant box: the soundboard is printed thin with internal ribbing to stay stiff under string tension without damping the panel, the back and sides are thicker for a rigid enclosure, and infill is kept low so the cavity stays a cavity. Layer orientation runs along the neck so the string load pulls along the layers rather than trying to split them, and the neck carries a channel for a stiffening rod. Bridge and nut are separate parts, printed dense, so the string break angle can be tuned without reprinting the body.

Mercedes-Benz star keychain

A small, precise piece modeled as a design exercise in relief and tolerance: the three-pointed star drawn as a sketch, revolved and extruded into a chamfered ring relief, with a filleted lanyard hole sized to clear a split ring. Printed at a fine layer height with the face flat on the bed so the top surface comes off the plate clean, and finished as a two-color part by pausing the print for a filament swap at the layer where the star meets the field. Personal, non-commercial print.

About & contact

I work across disciplines that usually sit in separate buildings. The same project might start as a parametric sketch in CAD, become a printed or welded part, get its behavior from a microcontroller reading analog sensors, and end with a bench measurement to prove it does what the design said it would.

The wet-lab work runs on the same instinct: follow the mechanism, control the conditions, then verify the result against a standard instead of assuming it. Whether the output is a launcher, a crystalline solid, or an instrument that holds tune, the habit is the same — design deliberately, build it yourself, and measure the outcome.
